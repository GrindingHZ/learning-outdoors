Learning Outdoors

Australians go into nature as adults. What happens before that?

Henson Zein, Monash FIT3179, October 2026

Bush walking is Australia's third most popular physical activity

AusPlay is the Australian Sports Commission's national survey of physical activity. A person counts as a participant if they did the activity at least once in the previous 12 months.

3.95madults bush walked in 2025, behind only walking and the gym

Only walking and the gym reach more people. Rank every activity AusPlay measures and the next nature activity, surfing, does not appear until 19th, with about thirteen times fewer people.

It happens everywhere, but not evenly

Two maps, two questions. The first asks where bush walking is most common as a share of adults. The second asks where the walkers actually are. They give different answers, because a high rate in a small state adds fewer people than an ordinary rate in a big one. Use the menu to redraw the first map for other nature activities.

27.5%of Tasmanian adults bush walk, the highest share of any state or territory

Bush walking is popular everywhere. Other nature activities cluster where the geography allows them: surf on the coasts, snow in the south east, fishing in the north.

An adult habit that lasts

Most physical activity fades with age. The grid below compares each age band with the activity's overall rate, so every row can be read on its own terms. Look for how long the green lasts along the bush walking row, then compare it with running.

21%of 35 to 44 year olds bush walk, the peak age band. At 55 to 64 it is still 20%

Nature activities mostly skew male. Bush walking and equestrian sports are the exceptions, and bush walking is the only one large enough to shape the national picture.

Children's activity happens at clubs and on the field

AusPlay measures children differently. For under 15s it counts only organised activity outside school hours. Free play and school sport are not included, so the children's figures below are a floor, not a total. Even so, the shape of the gap is clear.

1.1%of children bush walk in an organised group outside school, against 14% who play organised football

The same survey asks where the activity took place. For adults the answer is public space: parks, beaches and trails. For children it is most often a sports club, and schools matter more than they do for adults.

Why people say they go

AusPlay asks participants why they are active and lets them give more than one reason. Ranking the reasons within each age band shows what rises and what falls. Fun leads for adults under 25; health and fitness leads from 25 onwards. Follow the green line.

58%of active 55 to 64 year olds say being outdoors is one of their reasons

What students know

PISA is the OECD's international test of 15 year olds. Its 2025 round reports an environmental science score for 85 countries and economies. Australia sits well above the OECD average.

510points for Australian students, 30 above the OECD average and 11th of 85

What schools do

PISA also asks students what happens in class. Australian students report more chances to research environmental issues, and more assessment of what they know, than the OECD average. On one item they report much less.

4thlowest of 90 systems for the share of students taken on environmental field trips

Principals describe what their school offers. Australia stands out for garden programs and for sharing Indigenous knowledge about the environment. It falls behind on hands-on field work, such as biodiversity audits and field trips to waste facilities.

Knowledge is drifting, and interest goes with experience

PISA asked students in 2006, and again in 2025, whether they could explain environmental issues such as greenhouse gases. The arrows compare Australian students with the OECD average across those nineteen years.

−8.7points: fewer Australian students can explain greenhouse gases than in 2006

Across countries, students who report field trips are more likely to want an environmental career. This is a pattern between countries, not proof that trips cause interest. Australia and the United Kingdom, both high scorers on the world map above, sit in the bottom left. Drag across the chart to highlight a group.

The outdoor habit is real, it is adult, and it is learned late. The data on this page is the case for moving it earlier.

Notes on the data

AusPlay children's figures cover organised activity outside school hours only. AusPlay estimates carry margins of error of roughly 2.5 percentage points nationally and up to 7 points for the smaller jurisdictions, so Northern Territory and ACT figures for niche activities are approximate. PISA field trip and school activity items are self reported by students and principals. The OECD flags Canada, the Netherlands, New Zealand, Norway, the United States and Albania for sampling issues.

Method

Data cleaned with Python (pandas). Charts and maps built with Vega-Lite; each one is a separate JSON file in the js folder of this repository.
